\section{Motion}
\label{subsec:motion}
As described earlier, the displacement of a body is defined by: 
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{u = x - X}
    \label{eq:continuumdisplaceent}
\end{equation}

and the map from $\mathbf{X}$ to $\mathbf{x}$ is:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x} = \varphi(\mathbf{X},t)
    \label{eq:deformationmap}
\end{equation}

The velocity $\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{X}, t)$ is then, from \cref{eq:continuumdisplaceent} and \cref{eq:deformationmap}:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:materialvelocity}
    \mathbf{v}(\mathbf{X}, t)=\dfrac{\partial \varphi (\mathbf{X}, t)}{\partial t}=\dfrac{\partial \mathbf{u}(\mathbf{X}, t)}{\partial t} \equiv \dot{\mathbf{u}}
\end{equation}

in the same fashion, the acceleration is:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:materialacceleration}
    \mathbf{a}(\mathbf{X}, t)=\dfrac{\partial^2 \varphi (\mathbf{X}, t)}{\partial t^2}=\dfrac{\partial^2 \mathbf{u}(\mathbf{X}, t)}{\partial t^2}=\dfrac{\partial \mathbf{v}(\mathbf{X}, t)}{\partial t} \equiv \ddot{\mathbf{u}}
\end{equation}

\cref{eq:materialvelocity} and \cref{eq:materialacceleration} both relates to the spatial description of the body (point in the crowd). To define a quantity using the spatial description (person in the crowd), one has to define a material derivative using the chain rule. 

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:materialderiv-chain1}
    \dfrac{Dv_i(\mathbf{x},t)}{Dt} = 
    \dfrac{\partial v_i (\mathbf{x}, t)}{\partial t}
     + \dfrac{\partial v_i (\mathbf{x}, t)}{\partial x_j}
     \dfrac{\partial \varphi_i (\mathbf{X}, t)}{\partial t}
\end{equation}

The derivative of the deformation map $\varphi$ with respect to time, in the second term on the right hand side yields:

\begin{equation}
    \dfrac{\partial v_i (\mathbf{x}, t)}{\partial x_j}
     \dfrac{\partial \varphi_i (\mathbf{X}, t)}{\partial t} = v_j
\end{equation}

Which finally give:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:materialderiv-final}
    \dfrac{Dv_i(\mathbf{x},t)}{Dt} = 
    \dfrac{\partial v_i}{\partial t} + \dfrac{\partial v_i}{\partial x_j}v_j
\end{equation}

Or in matrix-vector from:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:materialderiv-final2}
    \dfrac{D \mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x},t)}{Dt} = 
    \dfrac{\partial \mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x}, t)}{\partial t} + \mathbf{v} \cdot \nabla \mathbf{v}
\end{equation}


which gives, in a more generic form:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:materialderiv-generic}
    \underbrace{\dfrac{D(\cdot)}{Dt} }_{\text{Material Derivative}}=
    \underbrace{\dfrac{\partial (\cdot)}{\partial t}}_{\text{Spatial term}} + 
    \underbrace{\mathbf{v } \cdot \nabla (\cdot)}_{\text{Convective/Adjective term}}
\end{equation}

\begin{example}{Ball drop}
    The position of a falling object, with respect to time and initial height ($Y$), is given by:

    $$
        \varphi(\mathbf{X}, t) = y = Y + v_0t - \tfrac{1}{2}gt^2
    $$
    With,
    $$
        \mathbf{u = x - X} 
    $$
    The definition of $\mathbf{u}$ becomes:
    $$
        \mathbf{u}(\mathbf{X}, t) = \varphi(\mathbf{X}, t) - \mathbf{X} 
    $$
    Since $\mathbf{X}=0=\text{constant}$, $\mathbf{u}$ becomes:
    $$
        \mathbf{u}(\mathbf{X}, t) = \varphi(\mathbf{X}, t) = Y + v_0t - \tfrac{1}{2}gt^2
    $$
    Then the velocity is: 
    $$
        \mathbf{v}(\mathbf{X},t) = \dfrac{\partial \mathbf{u}(\mathbf{X}, t)}{\partial t} = v_0 -gt
    $$
    and the acceleration:
    $$
        \mathbf{a}(\mathbf{X},t) = \dfrac{\partial \mathbf{v}(\mathbf{X}, t)}{\partial t} = -g
    $$

    Then what is the velocity field (velocity at any point and time)? From \cref{eq:materialderiv-generic}, we find that:

    $$
    \mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x}, t) = \dfrac{D \mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x},t)}{Dt} = 
    \dfrac{\partial \mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}, t)}{\partial t} + \mathbf{v} \cdot \nabla \mathbf{u}  
    $$
    Since every particle is subjected to the same gravitational field, the term $\mathbf{v \cdot \nabla u}$ vanishes meaning that:
    $$
    \mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x}, t) = \dfrac{D \mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x},t)}{Dt} = 
    \dfrac{\partial \mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}, t)}{\partial t}
    $$
    Thus,
    $$
    \mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x}, t) = \dfrac{\partial \mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}, t)}{\partial t} = v_0 - gt
    $$
    Which simply returns to the spatial description. However, it makes sense since if you suppose that every particles are in suspension initially and they drop, all of a sudden, then all of them accelerates at the same rate. Thus, at any point the velocity is the same for every particle.

    
\end{example}
